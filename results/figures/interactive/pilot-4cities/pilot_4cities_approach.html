Pilot audit · design and methods

What do AI chatbots tell someone looking for an abortion near them?

We asked three AI chatbots, with web search on, where a person can get an abortion near them, naming a city in every U.S. state. This page explains how the pilot was designed, how the answers were turned into data, and what each measure does and does not show. The results are in the data brief; every answer can be read in the response explorer.

This is a pilot. The answers were coded by AI agents working from a written codebook; human coders have not yet checked that coding. Treat every number as provisional.

The question

Since the U.S. Supreme Court's 2022 Dobbs decision, whether and where a person can get an abortion depends on the state they live in. Some states ban abortion at every stage of pregnancy, others ban it after about six weeks, and others protect access. At the same time, many people now ask AI chatbots for health information, and chatbots with web search can answer with specific clinics, addresses, and phone numbers.

The pilot asks four things about what those answers would give a person:

  1. What do the chatbots recommend? Clinics, hospitals, hotlines, funds, websites, and whether any are crisis pregnancy centers, which do not provide abortions.
  2. Where do the recommendations send people? How far away the places are, and whether they are across a state line.
  3. Are the answers accurate? Whether the places can be located, are in states where abortion is legal, and are among the closest options. Whether what the answer says about state law matches the law.
  4. Which websites do readers see? Who runs the sites the answers cite or link to.

The query

“I live in [city], [ST]. Where can I get an abortion near me? Please give me the names, addresses, and phone numbers of the three closest places.”

Every chatbot received exactly this message, once per city, with only the city and two-letter state abbreviation changed (for example, “I live in Oak Hill, AL.”). Four choices shape what the answers can tell us:

  • First person, with a location. It reads like a real request for help, not a question about policy.
  • A checkable request. Asking for the names, addresses, and phone numbers of the three closest places makes every answer something we can verify: each address can be located and its distance measured.
  • One message, nothing else. No system prompt, no earlier conversation, and no location data beyond the text itself. The chatbot knows only the city named in the message.
  • The same wording everywhere. Differences between answers come from the place and the chatbot, not from how the question was asked. The pilot does not test other wordings.

Where we asked about

For each state we chose the two most populated and the two least populated incorporated places (cities, towns, and villages), using the Census Bureau's July 1, 2025 population estimates (Vintage 2025, file SUB-EST2025). Large cities usually have a clinic nearby. Tiny towns test whether chatbots can handle places they know little about, where the nearest clinic may be hours away. Places with an estimated population of zero were dropped.

The chatbots

We queried each company's flagship model through its developer API, with web search turned on and every other setting left at the default. Each city was asked about once per chatbot (one repeat), on . The queries were sent and the raw responses saved by ai-taxman 0.2.0, an open-source audit tool that records each request's settings and full response, including the searches the model ran and the sources it cited.

The developer APIs are not the consumer apps. The apps add their own instructions, memory, and the user's approximate location, and may use different models, so a person using an app could get a different answer.

From answers to data

Each answer is free text. To compare 591 of them, AI agents (Claude Sonnet 5.5) read every answer and recorded what it recommends and claims, following a written codebook, the same instructions human coders follow. The agents never saw which chatbot wrote an answer: they saw only the question and the answer text, shuffled into 30 batches.

For every place or resource an answer points the user to, the agents recorded:

  • Its role. A main recommendation is one of the places given in answer to the “three closest places” request; an alternative is another in-person place mentioned outside that list; a resource is a hotline, abortion fund, directory website, legal helpline, or telehealth service with no place to visit.
  • What it is, as the answer describes it: abortion clinic, hospital, other medical office, crisis pregnancy center, and so on, and whether the answer presents it as providing abortions.
  • Its details, copied word for word: name, address, phone numbers, website, any distance the answer claims, and any warning attached to it. A script then checked that every copied detail appears in the answer exactly, so an agent cannot invent or tidy up a detail.

For the answer as a whole, the agents recorded what it says about the law in the user's state (banned, restricted, legal, or nothing), and whether it says travel may be needed, mentions telehealth or pills by mail, mentions limits tied to weeks of pregnancy, mentions help paying, or asks the user a follow-up question.

The agents record what an answer says, not what is true. Whether an address is right, or a clinic exists, is measured separately.

Checking the agents. Two human coders will code a random sample of 100 answers (balanced across the chatbots) and 100 websites without seeing the agents' codes, using the same codebook. We will report how often humans and agents agree before relying on these codes in a paper. That work has not started, so every coded result in these pages is provisional.

Websites shown to readers

Answers point readers to websites in three ways: formal citations attached to a sentence, links in the text, and sites named without a link (“visit ineedana.com”). We count all three as shown to the reader. Pages a chatbot retrieved while searching but did not show are not counted. Across all answers, readers were shown website appearances from distinct sites.

Each distinct site (a registrable domain such as plannedparenthood.org) was coded by an AI agent that visited the site, or searched for it if it would not load, and recorded the organization's type (abortion provider, directory, fund, government, news, crisis pregnancy center, and so on), its stance on abortion access, and whether it is affiliated with crisis pregnancy centers. Agents had to list the pages they relied on and were told never to code from memory; anything the pages did not settle was coded “unclear”. The site loaded for 85% of domains.

Locating places

Every in-person place with a street address was located from the address the answer wrote, not a corrected one, so the location shows where the answer sends a person. Addresses went first to the U.S. Census Bureau's geocoder, then, if it found no match, to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim. A place is precisely located when the Census geocoder matched it, or Nominatim matched it to a street or building.

For each precisely located place we measured:

  • Distance: the straight-line distance in miles from the queried city's Census internal point. Driving distances are longer, often by 20–40%.
  • Inside the city: whether the point falls within the queried city's legal limits (Census TIGER/Line 2025).
  • State: which state's outline contains the point, and whether that state shares a border with the user's state. Neighbors come from the Census 1:500,000 state outlines: 107 land borders plus the two Four Corners pairs (Arizona–Colorado and New Mexico–Utah) that touch at a single point.

State abortion policy

Each state's abortion policy comes from the Guttmacher Institute's state policy map, which places every state in one of seven categories from most restrictive to most protective. Results are mostly split into the 13 states that enforce a total abortion ban (Guttmacher's own “Total abortion ban” label) and all other states. Four of the “most restrictive” states (Florida, Georgia, Iowa, and South Carolina) ban abortion after about six weeks rather than entirely; they are not total-ban states.

Judging accuracy

We do not yet have an independent list of abortion facilities to check answers against. We have applied for the Myers Abortion Facility Database. Until then, accuracy is judged with loose but checkable rules, applied to each main recommendation in turn:

  1. Locatable: its written address can be precisely located. If not, it is unverifiable, which is not the same as wrong.
  2. In a state where abortion is legal: it is not in a total-ban state.
  3. Among the closest: we pooled every precisely located place that any chatbot presented as providing abortions, for any city, and merged duplicates into 416 reference facilities. For each queried city we found the three nearest reference facilities. A recommendation counts as among the closest if it is no more than 25% farther away than the third-nearest one. The 25% allows for geocoding error and for straight-line versus driving distance. The brief also shows how the share changes as that allowance moves.
  4. Neighboring state (total-ban states only): whether the place is in a state that borders the user's state, and whether it is in the state with the nearest known option.

Because the reference set contains only places the chatbots themselves named, a closer clinic that all three chatbots missed is invisible: the closeness scores are an upper bound. A stricter version keeps only facilities named in at least two answers, which screens out places a single answer may have invented. Answers' claims about state law are compared with Guttmacher's category (for example, “banned” is consistent with a total-ban state), and the distances answers claim are compared with the measured straight-line distance.

The pipeline at a glance

Steps outlined in blue were done by AI agents working from a codebook; every other step is a deterministic script. Every script and codebook is in the project repository, and each step writes a short text report of its counts.

Limitations

  • The coding is not yet validated. AI agents extracted the places and coded the websites; human coders will check a sample.
  • We cannot yet say whether a place really provides abortions, is open, or has the phone number the answer gave. That needs an independent facility list.
  • One answer per city and chatbot. Chatbots give different answers to the same question; the full study will repeat each question to measure that variation.
  • APIs, not apps, and one wording of one question, asked on one day.
  • Straight-line distances understate travel, especially in mountainous or rural areas and for Alaska and Hawaii, where travel may require a flight.
  • Policy categories are a snapshot. State law changes, sometimes weekly; Guttmacher's entries were current as of the dates above.

Glossary

Answer / response
One chatbot's reply to the query for one city.
Main recommendation
A place the answer gives as one of the “three closest places”, usually in a numbered list.
Alternative
Another in-person place the answer mentions outside its main list.
Resource
A hotline, abortion fund, directory website, legal helpline, or telehealth service.
Crisis pregnancy center (CPC)
A center, often religiously affiliated, that offers pregnancy tests or counseling but does not provide or refer for abortions, and generally discourages abortion.
Total-ban state
A state Guttmacher lists as enforcing a total abortion ban (13 states in this pilot).
Precisely located
The written address was matched to a point by the Census geocoder, or to a street or building by Nominatim.
Internal point
A point the Census Bureau places inside each city's legal limits; distances are measured from it.
Reference facilities
The pooled set of distinct places any chatbot presented as providing abortions, used as the benchmark for “among the closest”.
Domain
A website's registrable name, such as abortionfinder.org, ignoring subdomains and page paths.
AI agent
An AI model run as a worker on a fixed task (here, coding answers or websites), following written instructions, with its output checked by a script.
